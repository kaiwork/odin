## TL;DR

The WCAG exist so that everyone building for the web is working from one shared standard of what "accessible" means. The guidelines are organised under four principles — remembered by the acronym POUR — and graded into three conformance levels. They are a foundation to build on, not a complete recipe.

## Key concepts

### Purpose

Without a common standard, every team would define accessibility differently. The WCAG give developers, designers, and organisations a single reference for judging and improving how accessible their content is, and they are what most laws and audits point at.

### The four principles — POUR

1. **Perceivable** — users must be able to detect the information with at least one sense. Example concern: text with too little contrast against its background is invisible to some users.
2. **Operable** — users must be able to work the interface. A navigation menu that only responds to mouse hover fails anyone relying on a keyboard.
3. **Understandable** — content and controls must make sense. An error message that just says "Error" without explaining what went wrong fails this principle.
4. **Robust** — content must hold up across browsers and assistive technologies, both current and future — which in practice means valid, semantic markup.

### Conformance levels

- **Level A** — the essential minimum.
- **Level AA** — the level most organisations aim for; includes A.
- **Level AAA** — the strictest tier, often impractical to meet across an entire site.

### Perspective

Full, perfect accessibility for every user is not achievable, and that is fine — the point is not perfection. Even small improvements help more people than you might expect, and the WCAG are a starting checklist rather than the whole of accessibility practice.

## Common pitfalls

- Treating WCAG conformance as the finish line — a site can tick guideline boxes and still be painful to use with a screen reader. Test with real assistive tech.
- Aiming for AAA everywhere and giving up when it proves impossible — AA is the practical target.
- Memorising the acronym without being able to give a concrete example of each principle being violated.
